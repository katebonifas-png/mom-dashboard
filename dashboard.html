She can't reliably retain or relay information — so the window for doing this the easy way is closing. Anything needing her consent, her voice on a call, or her signature should happen in the next few days. After that the alternative is guardianship: slow, expensive, court-supervised. Sit down with her and make the calls together — most institutions will take verbal authorization while she's on the line and then add you permanently. See Digital access on the Checklist tab. Two cautions: don't have her agree to things she doesn't actually follow in the moment, and don't create a single point of failure — if all her MFA routes to your phone, store backup codes somewhere else and name someone who can reach them if something happens to you.
Before you consolidate anything: you have a durable POA, which means you can manage her accounts — but managing is not the same as merging. Do not retitle her accounts into your name or add yourself as a joint owner. If Medicaid is ever needed, that reads as a transfer and can trigger a penalty period. Keep her money in her name, with you acting as agent under the POA.
Check the Type column against reality. The withdrawal-tax haircut on the Overview only applies to accounts typed IRA, 401(k) or Annuity. If none of her money is actually tax-deferred, make sure nothing is mis-typed — a wrong type here understates how much she has available for care, and therefore understates her runway.
On her debts. The SBA loan and the credit cards are not the same kind of problem. Credit card companies cannot touch Social Security — federal law protects it, and if her assets are being consumed by care she is effectively judgment-proof. But a personally guaranteed SBA loan goes to the Treasury Offset Program, which can reach her Social Security without a court order and adds a ~30% surcharge. The exposure is capped though: the offset is the lesser of 15% of the monthly benefit or the amount above $750 — the first $750/month is protected outright. On an $1,800 benefit that is $270/month. Real money out of her care budget, but calculate the actual figure before treating it as a catastrophe. So the first question isn't how to pay it — it's whether she personally guaranteed it at all. COVID EIDL loans under $200,000 generally required no personal guarantee, in which case the debt belongs to the closed business. See the Her debts section on the Checklist tab.

Account inventory

Investment and retirement account balances go here, not in Income — they fund her care by being spent down, not by paying her monthly. The Type you pick matters: IRA, 401(k), and Annuity balances get reduced by the withdrawal tax rate you set on the Overview tab, credit cards and loans subtract, and life insurance / safe deposit box are held out of the runway as not readily spendable. Check for accounts she's forgotten: old 401(k)s from past employers, credit union savings, savings bonds, life insurance with cash value.

Where her things went

Keep this for the Medicaid file, not for the money. A caseworker reviewing 60 months may ask what happened to her belongings and her car — a dated log with values answers it in one page instead of a scramble. Log anything sold, donated, or given to family, with the date and what it was worth.

Two things that should lower the stress here. Household goods and personal effects are generally exempt assets for Medicaid — donating furniture, dishes and clothing is not the kind of transfer that creates a penalty. And a $600 car is genuinely de minimis: at roughly three days of care, the hours it would take to sell it privately are probably worth more than the difference. Donating is defensible. What matters is the paper, not the price.

For the car specifically: get a dated receipt from the charity showing the vehicle and its fair market value, and keep the title transfer paperwork. If they sell it for more than $500 they must send you a Form 1098-C. Record the odometer and take a photo of the car before it goes. That packet is what closes the question if anyone ever asks where a titled asset went.
Anything of real value should be sold at fair price, not gifted. Proceeds go into her account. Small personal items to family are unlikely to matter; jewelry, collectibles, or anything with a title are the ones to document carefully.

Everything that has to happen

Add a due date to anything time-sensitive and it'll surface above. Add your own tasks at the bottom of any section.

Two that trip up almost everyone: schedule utility shutoffs for after the cleanout, not for move day — people cancel the power and then can't see to clear the closets. And never sign the facility agreement as a personally responsible party; sign as agent under POA, or her bill can become your debt.
Since she rents: check your state's senior lease-termination statute before paying any early-exit penalty. A majority of states let a tenant 62 or older who is moving into assisted living end a lease early regardless of its terms — typically on 30–60 days' written notice with a physician's letter and proof of admission. Separately, federal fair housing law lets a tenant with a qualifying disability request early termination as a reasonable accommodation. Landlords don't always volunteer this. It can be worth several thousand dollars.
Everything here happens after you're back in LA. The move is the loud part; this is the part that determines whether the next two years go well.

The Medicaid application

This is the main event of the next several months. Applications commonly take 45–90 days to process, and states review the full 60 months of financial history before approving.

Timing it: you apply as she approaches Oregon's asset limit of $2,000, not after she hits it — the processing gap is the danger zone, and facilities expect payment during it. Watch the runway on the Overview tab and start the application roughly 4–6 months before the money runs out. Ask the elder law attorney to name the month.
